How it works

  1. Order the layers: the number with the fewest places left goes next (checked again after every layer). The other option is your rule: the most repeated number first.
  2. Start from the givens: the number sits on its given cells; in every other box it starts on the first free cell.
  3. Move inside the boxes until the layer fits: it covers its givens, avoids the grey cells already taken, and still has no repeats in rows or columns. Each move is a word: 5·b4 r4c2→r5c3 = move the 5 inside box 4.
  4. Lock the layer: its cells turn grey for every later layer.
  5. Choice or forced? A layer with only one possible placement is • forced — it can't be the mistake. A layer with several is a ◆ choice; the first choice is outlined in red.
  6. Dead end? When a number has a box with no free cell, look at which layers took the cells it needed there, and jump straight back to the most recent of them that made a choice — every layer in between is undone without trying its other placements, because it didn't cause the problem. Fixing the cause fixes everything after it. This still finds the solution of any valid puzzle.
  7. Spot the mistake early: after each layer, every remaining number must still have a complete placement — not just a free cell in each box. This catches the layer that causes a dead end right away, and halves the placements tried.
  8. Try both, keep the better (the default): the layers are built once in each order — fewest places first, and your most-repeated-first rule — and the build that went back less is shown. Each order wins on different puzzles.
  9. Method: option layers — instead of choosing a placement and hoping, first build every option layer of the 2 most repeated numbers (adding the next when stuck), AND and cut them against each other, and 🔒 lock a number as soon as one option is left. The board then builds each locked layer in the order it was locked. Locked layers are proven, so they never need a go-back; a guess is made only if the cuts stop.
  10. The other two options are for comparison: going back one layer at a time (slower), and always going straight to the first choice (fast, but it can jump past the solution when the real mistake is a later choice).

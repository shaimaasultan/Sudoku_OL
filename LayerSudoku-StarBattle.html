The chips are the units — rows (R), columns (C) and regions (letters) — with the number of options each still has (– = not built yet, ✓ = locked). The unit being worked on is outlined on the board; its cells are shaded by the share of its options that put a star there.

How it works — the same method as your Sudoku layers

  1. Units: every row, column and region must hold exactly the given number of stars, and stars may not touch — not even diagonally. A unit here is like a number's layer in Sudoku; each cell is shared by one row, one column and one region, like a pillar.
  2. Givens (optional): some stars can be placed at the start (dark) — they and their neighbours are fixed before the method begins, which makes the puzzle easier.
  3. Option layers of a unit: every way to put its stars in cells that are still free, not touching each other.
  4. Extra units (optional, to compare): your 2×2 boxes tiling the board — at most one star each — or bands of two neighbouring rows or columns, which must hold exactly twice the stars of one row.
  5. Thinnest units first: start with the 2 units that have the fewest options; when the cuts stop, open the next thinnest unit.
  6. Cut options that use a cell already known to be empty, or miss a star already placed.
  7. AND: a cell starred in all options of a unit gets a star (and its 8 neighbours become empty); a cell in no option is empty.
  8. Full unit: once a unit has all its stars, its other cells are empty. 🔒 Lock a unit when one option is left.
  9. All units together: if every unit is built and the cuts stop, test each option of the unlocked units on its own — if assuming it makes some unit lose all its options, it is cut. Nothing is guessed.
  10. Only if even that stops: guess an option of the unit with the fewest options and go back if it fails.

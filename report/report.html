1. Summary

This report describes and tests the option-layer method, an extension of the author's layer-propagation Sudoku solver. Each number's layer is described by the list of its whole placements ("options"). Options that cannot fit are cut; cells used by every option are certain; a layer with one option left is locked. Layers are built thinnest first, pairs and groups of 3–4 layers are checked together, and a guess is made only as the last step.

The same three ideas — units, options, pillars — were used for eight puzzles (Sudoku, Latin squares, Killer Sudoku, KenKen, Nonograms, Star Battle, N-Queens and map colouring) and for a new optimisation problem, Star Cost: Star Battle with a weight on every cell, where the cheapest placement is wanted.

Main findings.

  • Option layers solved every 9×9 Sudoku without a guess; the 3/4-number group check was needed for 37 of them. MRV backtracking needed about 1,186 search nodes per puzzle at 22 givens.
  • For N-Queens with givens (up to 30×30) and sparse Nonograms, option layers did clearly better than classic search: row backtracking failed on 5 boards within 5 million nodes, and a line solver guessed on 16 Nonograms.
  • A lean version that keeps only build, cut and AND (starting from the top 2 layers) is 2–160× faster than the full engine — the gap grows with the board. Started from the numbers with the fewest open cells, it had the fastest median time of all methods on every board from 9×9 to 30×30, ahead of Dancing Links and MRV backtracking. It pays with some guesses, which the full engine avoids.
  • For Star Cost, a Lagrangian ("weight descent") bound proves answers that the simple bound cannot. The author's lock-cheapest rule with look-ahead, followed by Unstick (a large-neighbourhood rebuild), came within about 5–6% of the best on large boards, ahead of simulated annealing and random-restart descent.

2. Background: the layer-propagation papers

This work continues three papers by the author, A Scalable Layer–Propagation and MRV Branching Method for Solving and Generating Large N×N Sudoku (versions 1–3). The papers describe a hybrid solver for any board size, from 4×4 up to 36×36, with rectangular boxes for sizes such as 6×6 (2×3 boxes) and 12×12 (3×4 boxes). It has five parts:

  1. Layer-based propagation. The board is seen as N stacked layers, one per number. Layer d marks where d can still go. A placed number clears its row, column and box in its own layer, and clears its cell in every other layer.
  2. Single-missing deduction. A row, column or box with one place left for a number gets it at once.
  3. MRV candidate selection. When logic stops, the cell with the fewest candidates (minimum remaining values) is chosen.
  4. Shallow branching with forbidden-value learning. A guess that fails is remembered as forbidden, so the same dead end is not tried twice.
  5. Early conflict detection, and a generator that removes clues with symmetry and structure checks, so large puzzles stay solvable.

The papers report results for 9×9 to 36×36 at 25% and 35% clues, and compare the method with pure backtracking and human-style logic solvers.

The step described in this report grows out of the first idea: if each number is a layer, then each layer can be described by the list of whole placements it could still take. We call these placements options. Working with whole options, instead of single cells, lets the solver see facts that single-cell logic misses. It also carries over, almost unchanged, to other puzzles and to optimisation.

3. The option-layer method

3.1 Units, options and pillars

Every puzzle in this report is described with three words:

  • Unit. A part of the puzzle that must be completed as a whole. In Sudoku, a unit is one number's layer: the nine places of the digit 5.
  • Option. One complete way to fill a unit. For the digit 5 in a 9×9 Sudoku, an option is a set of nine cells with one cell in every row, column and box. (Such a set is also called a template.) At the start there are 46,656 options per digit; givens reduce this quickly.
  • Pillar. The thing the units share. In Sudoku this is the cell: every cell must be used by exactly one digit's option.

3.2 The rules

The method repeats a few simple rules until the board is solved. Each rule is cheap to explain, and every step it takes can be shown to a person as a reason.

RuleWhat it does
CutRemove every option that uses a cell which is already taken, or a cell whose candidate for this unit has been removed.
AND (certain cells)If a cell is used by every remaining option of a unit, place it.
NarrowIf a cell is used by no remaining option of a unit, remove that unit's candidate from the cell. This is the OR of the options.
SinglesA cell with one candidate left, or a unit with one place left in a row, column or box, is filled (the papers' single-missing deduction).
🔒 LockA unit with exactly one option left is locked: its whole placement is written at once.
Pair checkTwo units that share cells must have options that fit together. An option of unit A that clashes with every option of unit B is cut.
Group checkThe same test for 3 or 4 units together: an option is cut if no choice for the other units in the group fits beside it.
Probe (all units together)Used in puzzles without a cheap group check: assume one option, run all the rules, and cut the option if a contradiction appears.
GuessOnly when nothing else works: try the smallest unit's options one by one, with backtracking (the papers' MRV branching).

3.3 Thinnest first: building layers one at a time

Listing every option of every unit can be expensive. The method therefore builds option layers incrementally. It starts with the two units that have the fewest options, and adds the next thinnest unit only when the checks stop making progress. When two units have the same count, the one with fewer free cells in a box goes first. On most puzzles the board is solved before the larger layers are ever listed; this is the "option layers built" count in Section 5.

function solveOptionLayers(board):
  units ← all units, sorted by estimated option count (fewest first)
  active ← first two units;  build their option lists
  loop
    changed ← applyRules(active)          // cut, AND, narrow, singles, lock
    if board solved: return board
    if changed: continue
    if pairCheck(active) cut something: continue
    if groupCheck(active, size 3..4) cut something: continue
    if some unit not active: active ← active + nextThinnest(); continue
    if probe(active) cut something: continue   // puzzles without a group check
    u ← unit with fewest options                   // guess last (MRV)
    for each option o of u:
      if solveOptionLayers(board with o locked) succeeds: return it
    return fail

3.4 Why whole options see more

Single-cell logic looks at one cell or one line at a time. An option joins all nine places of a digit, so a single cut can reach across the whole board. Classic human techniques such as X-Wing, Swordfish and Jellyfish for one digit are special cases of the AND/narrow rule on one layer: they fall out automatically, without being coded. The pair check covers many two-digit patterns, and the 3/4-number group check covers the remaining hard cases in our tests (Section 5.1).

A guess-free explanation

Every step before the first guess is a reason that a person can check: "no option of the 7s uses this cell", "every option of the 3s uses this cell", "this option of the 2s leaves no room for any option of the 8s". The pages call these steps the sure part of the log, and the hint button only uses them.

5. Solving experiments

Set-up. Every page was loaded in a headless Microsoft Edge browser and driven by a small script (Appendix). The browser's random numbers were replaced by a seeded generator, so each puzzle can be made again from its seed. Every method ran on the same puzzles, one after the other, on one core. Every solution was checked against the rules. Times are browser milliseconds; the timer steps in units of 0.1 ms, so very small times are rounded.

Methods compared.

  • Option layers — the method of Section 3, as used in the pages.
  • Option layers, pairs only — the same without the 3/4-number group check, to measure what that check adds.
  • Layer Builder — the author's earlier layer-propagation solver with MRV branching (the papers' method).
  • MRV backtracking — the classic solver popularised by Norvig: propagate, then branch on the cell with the fewest candidates.
  • Dancing Links — Knuth's Algorithm X on the exact-cover form of Sudoku, choosing the column with the fewest rows.
  • For the other puzzles: a DP line solver (Nonograms), cage-aware MRV backtracking (Killer, KenKen), row-by-row backtracking (Star Battle, N-Queens) and DSATUR (maps).

Where option layers win. Two results stand out. First, N-Queens with givens: at 30×30 the option layers took a median of 29 ms, while row backtracking took a median of 430 ms and failed to finish 4 of 10 boards within 5 million nodes. The attack rule empties whole diagonals at once, and locking a row with one cell left spreads far. Second, sparse Nonograms: a classic line solver settles each line on its own and then must guess. On sparse 15×15 puzzles it guessed in 14 of 25 (1,341 guesses in total). Option layers, with the "all lines together" probe, solved every one without a guess (Figure 5.9).

Where classic search wins. For small, dense puzzles (KenKen, Star Battle 1★, Latin squares), simple backtracking finds the answer in well under a millisecond, before option lists would even be built. Option layers pay for listing whole placements up front; that cost is repaid only when the search tree would otherwise be large. This is the engine written for the pages, with its step log; Section 5.4 shows what the core rules cost on their own.

5.4 Lean option layers: only build, cut and AND

The option-layer engine used so far records every step for the pages and adds the pair check, the 3/4-number group check and the probe. To see what the core of the method costs, a second engine was written with only four rules:

  1. Build — start with only the top 2 (or 3) numbers, chosen as the ones with the fewest open cells (or the most repeated); list their options. After the results below, fewest open cells became the default in the Speed page. Add the next number only when nothing else changes. Layers are never all built at once.
  2. Cut — remove options that use a cell the number can no longer take.
  3. AND / OR — a cell in every option is certain; a cell in no option is removed; one option left = locked.
  4. Pillar — a cell that only one number can still reach gets that number.

A guess (fewest options first) is made only when every layer is built and nothing changes. The data is kept in bit sets, so a cut or an AND is a few machine-word operations. There is no step log, no pair or group check, and no probe. The engine is in the new page LayerSudoku-Speed.html; short solves are repeated and averaged so that times below the 0.1 ms timer step are still measured.

The full engine is for explaining: no guesses and a reason for every step. The lean engine is for speed: the same layers, cuts and ANDs, at the speed of the fastest classic solvers.

What the lean test shows.

  • The core is fast, and the gap grows with the board. Lean option layers were 2× faster than the full engine on 4×4, 12–15× on 9×9, 29× on 12×12, 57× on 16×16, 80× on 20×20 and about 160× on 30×30. The full engine spends its time on the step log, the pair and group checks and on listing more layers (on 30×30 a median of about 59,000 options, against about 900 for lean).
  • It matches or beats the classic solvers. Started from the numbers with the fewest open cells, lean had the fastest median on every board from 9×9 to 30×30 (level with MRV on 9×9 medium and with Dancing Links on 12×12); at 30×30 it took 3.4 ms against 4.8 ms for Dancing Links and 9.5 ms for MRV. Started from the most repeated number, it was fastest on 9×9 hard, 12×12, 16×16 hard and 20×20, while Dancing Links was slightly faster on 16×16 medium (0.60 vs 0.64 ms) and 30×30 (4.8 vs 5.6 ms). Plain MRV wins only on 4×4 and 6×6, where there is almost nothing to search; from 12×12 up lean was 1.7–6× faster than MRV.
  • The price is some guessing and a longer tail. Without the pair and group checks, lean guessed on 23 of 100 hard 9×9 puzzles and 18 of 30 12×12 puzzles (the full engine: none); on the bigger boards, which keep about half of the cells as givens, it rarely guessed (2 of 10 at 16×16, 1 of 20 at 20×20, none at 30×30). The guesses are cheap, but they make the slowest puzzles slower: the slowest 20×20 took 14 ms against 1.4 ms for Dancing Links, and the average on 12×12 and 20×20 (0.31 and 1.6 ms) was higher than Dancing Links' (0.24 and 1.1 ms).
  • The starting rule matters more on big boards. On 9×9 and 12×12, starting with the top 2 or 3 layers, by most repeated or by fewest open cells, changed the median by only about 10%. On 20×20 the fewest-open-cells start was about 20% faster, and on 30×30 about 40% faster (3.4 vs 5.6 ms).

6. Optimisation: map colouring and Star Cost

A logic puzzle asks for one answer that fits. An optimisation problem asks for the best of many answers. The option lists help in both: they say which answers are still possible, and they give cheap limits on how good an answer can be.

6.1 Map colouring: the fewest colours

Maps were made by growing random regions (Voronoi cells) and joining neighbours. The page finds the fewest colours k by trying k = (largest group of mutual neighbours), k+1, … and proving each smaller k impossible. Each try is an option-layer solve: regions are units, colours are options, and a shared border is the pillar. A triangle of neighbouring regions is checked as a 3-unit group.

6.2 Star Cost: a weights layer on Star Battle

Star Cost adds a weights layer on top of a Star Battle board. Every cell has a cost from 1 to 9. The stars must still obey the rules (K stars in every row, column and region, no two touching), and the goal is the placement with the lowest total cost. This is like choosing where to put K facilities per area, when neighbouring sites may not both be used.

Exact methods (branch and bound)

  • Simple bound. For each family of units (rows, columns, regions), add up the cheapest option of every unit; the largest of these three sums is a lower limit on any answer.
  • Weight descent bound. Put a price on each column and region, fold the prices into the row costs, and take the cheapest row options. Adjust the prices by gradient steps to raise the limit. (This is Lagrangian relaxation with subgradient steps.)
  • Branching. Either on the unit with the fewest options, or on the cheapest free cell (the author's rule: try the cheapest star first).

Fast heuristics

  • ⚡ Cheapest-first. Put stars on the lowest weights first, and move to the next cell only when the no-touch rule or a unit count fails; the option layers keep every step legal.
  • 🔒 Lock cheapest (look-ahead m). Try the m cheapest free cells; for each, finish the board cheapest-first and keep the cell whose finish is lowest. Lock it and repeat.
  • ⛰ Downhill. From any answer, apply the best "rectangle swap" (two stars trade columns) until no swap lowers the cost.
  • Unstick. When downhill stops, free about 60% of the rows (or columns), and rebuild them exactly with the option layers and bounds. Keep the result if it is cheaper. This is large-neighbourhood search.
  • For comparison: random start + downhill (5 restarts) and simulated annealing (20,000 swap steps).

7.1 What is new here

  • One vocabulary for many puzzles. Units, options and pillars describe Sudoku, Latin squares, Killer, KenKen, Nonograms, Star Battle, N-Queens, map colouring and a weighted optimisation problem. The rules and the step-by-step display are shared.
  • Thinnest-first incremental building of option lists, so that only the needed layers are ever listed (Figure 5.4).
  • Group checks on 3 or 4 units, which in our tests removed every remaining guess on 9×9 Sudoku.
  • Explainable, guess-free solving: every step before a guess is a checkable reason, and the hint uses only these steps.
  • A lean engine with the speed of classic search: build (top 2 layers first), cut and AND on bit sets is enough to match Dancing Links and MRV on boards from 9×9 up to 30×30.
  • From solving to optimising: the same option lists give lower bounds and moves for a weighted problem (Star Cost), with cheapest-first, lock-cheapest with look-ahead, and Unstick.

8. When option layers win and when MRV wins

The Few Givens page races the methods on the same puzzles made by filling a random fraction of a solved grid. Few givens usually leave many answers, so these tests show a side of the problem that carved puzzles (exactly one answer) do not. The results below are from 9×9 unless the size is named.

The short rule

What decides the winner is not the fill %, and not only the number of answers. It is how many wrong choices still look right to simple candidate counting.

  • MRV wins when the answer is easy to see: a huge number of answers (almost any choice works) or one answer that plain counting finds. MRV has nothing to build, so its low start-up cost wins.
  • Option layers win when wrong choices look right: one or a few answers on hard puzzles, and the middle zone on large boards. The layers see hidden reasons (a number's whole placement) that MRV cannot, so they skip the long wrong paths.

8.4 Summary

  • Best for MRV: small boards, very few givens (many thousands of answers), or many givens (the answer is easy to see).
  • Best for option layers: large boards (12×12 and up), hard carved puzzles with exactly one answer, and the middle zone of a few hundred answers (about 25 – 40% fill), where MRV and Dancing Links can run for millions of steps.
  • Fill % is only a proxy. The same fill can give an easy puzzle or a hard one. The number of answers is a better guide, and the best guide is how many wrong choices simple counting cannot rule out.

9. The cube as a tensor, and Dancing Layers

The option-layer cube (row × column × number) can be held as one N×N×N true/false tensor: a point is true while that number can still go in that cell. Every rule of Sudoku says "exactly one true point on each line of the cube" — each cell, and each number in each row, column and box. This chapter tests four engines built on that view, on the Tensor Layers page (LayerSudoku-Tensor.html), against the lean engine and the classic solvers.

9.1 Four engines

  • Tensor OL. The tensor is kept in four views at once (cell, row, column, box), each line packed into one 32-bit word, so a line with no point (a dead end) or one point (a naked or hidden single) is found in one pass. Layers can be cut in four directions: a number layer (a horizontal slice: the templates of the lean method), or a row, column or box layer (vertical slices: every way to give its open cells different numbers). The thinnest layer in any allowed direction is built next — top 2 at the start, one more when stuck, never all; a layer over the cap (2,000 options) waits until its size bound shrinks 8 times. Cut and AND / OR are the same in every direction: row, column and box layers find naked and hidden sets, number layers find fish patterns.
  • Matrix OL (matmul). The tensor is one flat 0/1 vector x. The line sums are one product A·x with the fixed line matrix A. All layers of one direction are stacked into one option × point matrix M; one pass is alive = (M·(1−x) = 0) (cut) and count = aliveᵀ·M (count 0 → remove, OR; count = the live options of its layer → place, AND). Every row, column and box layer is built in full at the start; only the top 2 or 3 number layers are built.
  • Shared number patterns. A template is a pattern — one cell per row, column and box — that does not depend on the number. So the thinnest numbers are built in one walk over the patterns, each pattern kept once with a mask of the numbers it fits. Instead of cutting the lists again, each point keeps a support count (the live patterns that use it) and each number its live count; removing a point touches only the patterns through that cell. Support 0 removes a point; support equal to the live count places it. After a failed guess the changes are undone from a trail, so the list is never scanned or copied again. When stuck, one more number joins the shared set.
  • Dancing Layers. Dancing Links (exact cover) whose rows are whole templates. For the top 2 numbers whose layer fits the cap, every template is one row covering its N cells and the column "number d"; every other number keeps one row per possible cell (the cell and "d in its row / column / box"). Choosing a row unlinks every clashing template and candidate, and going back links them again — the layers are cached and toggled, never cut, copied or rebuilt. Every step works on the column with the fewest live rows: a cell, a whole number layer, or a number in a row, column or box. The same code with no templates ("exact cover, no templates") is plain Dancing Links on the candidates the givens leave open, and shows what the templates add.

9.3 What the results show

  • Toggling instead of re-cutting is the biggest gain. On open boards Dancing Layers is far faster than every option-layer engine that cuts and copies its lists: 9×9 random 20% 0.39 ms against 5.00 (lean) and 8.89 (shared patterns); 16×16 random 40% 1.26 ms against 12.1 (shared) and 30.8 (lean). It also beats the Speed page's Dancing Links on 16×16 random 40% (1.71 ms) and on 9×9 random 34% (0.29 ms).
  • Templates help on tight boards, not on open ones. Against the same code with no templates: 9×9 hard 0.13 vs 0.19 ms, 9×9 random 34% 0.11 vs 0.13, 16×16 hard 0.82 vs 1.19 — but 9×9 random 20% 0.39 vs 0.28, and 16×16 random 40% 1.26 vs 1.08. A template settles N cells in one choice when the lists are short; on open boards the extra rows slow every column choice. With templates for all numbers (top 9) the short checks were several times slower.
  • 16×16 hard (one answer): option-layer engines are fastest. Tensor with all four directions 0.44 ms and shared patterns 0.58 ms, against Dancing Links 1.35 and MRV 4.87 ms. On hard puzzles the extra directions pay (tensor with number layers only: 0.79 ms); on open boards they cost (16×16 random 40%: 37.9 vs 23.2 ms).
  • MRV is still fastest on every 9×9 case (50 µs on hard puzzles, 38 µs at 34%): with many answers almost any guess works, and MRV guesses cheaply. On 16×16 random 40% it is the slowest (260 ms, 2 of 10 unsolved within 5 million steps).
  • Shared patterns beat number layers built one at a time on open boards (9×9 random 20%: 8.89 vs 10.5 ms; 16×16 random 40%: 12.1 vs 23.2), but every list-cutting engine stays far behind Dancing Layers there.
  • Building the vertical layers in full (the matrix engines) does not pay on the CPU. They are the slowest or close to it in every case — on 16×16 hard the full lists hold 6,000–21,000 options, and every pass multiplies all of them. Their two products fit a GPU, which would only pay on large boards.

10. Discussion, limits and related work

10.1 What the experiments show

  • Guess-free solving is the main strength. Across 1,066 logic puzzles of seven types, option layers solved 1,048 (98%) with no guess at all, and all of them correctly. All 500 9×9 Sudoku, including the hardest at 22 givens, needed no guess.
  • Group checks matter. Pairs alone left 37 of 500 Sudoku unfinished; the 3/4-number group check finished all of them.
  • Option layers are strongest when classic search is weakest: N-Queens with givens at 20×20 and 30×30, and sparse Nonograms.
  • Speed depends on the engine, not the idea. The full engine, written for display, is about 5× slower than Dancing Links on 9×9 and about 180× slower on 30×30. The lean engine — the same layers, cuts and ANDs on bit sets, with no step log and no pair or group check — had the fastest median on every board from 9×9 to 30×30 when it started from the fewest open cells.
  • Explaining and speed pull in different directions. The pair and group checks remove every guess but cost time; without them the lean engine guesses on about a quarter of hard 9×9 puzzles and still finishes first.

10.2 Limits

  • Option lists can be large. A 16×16 digit has millions of templates on an empty board. The thinnest-first rule avoids listing large layers, but a sparse, large board can still be slow (16×16 median 24 ms, with up to several thousand lists built).
  • Star Battle with 2 stars has many options per unit; the 10×10 2★ puzzles took about 1 s at the median, and up to 9 s.
  • Small numbers of puzzles at the biggest sizes (12 at 16×16, 10 at 30×30 N-Queens, 11 at 10×10 2★ Star Battle) because making unique hard puzzles is slow. Results there are indicative.
  • Browser timing has a 0.1 ms step, and all runs used one machine. Only relative times should be read from the charts.
  • The group check is exact for the units it looks at, but it does not look at every group of 3 or 4. A puzzle can exist that needs a guess; one 12×12 Sudoku did (3 guesses).

10.3 Related work

The idea of listing whole placements of a digit is known in the Sudoku community as the pattern overlay method or templates. Exact cover and Dancing Links (Knuth, 2000) give the same set picture for search. Nonogram line solvers compute the overlap of all placements of a line by dynamic programming. Constraint programming uses table constraints (lists of allowed tuples) and singleton arc consistency, which is close to the probe rule. Lagrangian relaxation for lower bounds and large-neighbourhood search (Shaw, 1998) are standard in operations research. The contribution of this work is to join these ideas in one layered method, with thinnest-first building and group checks, and to show that the same units-options-pillars view carries across eight puzzles and into optimisation, while keeping every step explainable.

10.4 Future work

  • Adding a cheap pair check to the lean engine only when it is about to guess, to keep its speed and remove most guesses.
  • Choosing which groups of 3–4 units to check by a score, instead of in order.
  • The "language" of moves: grading puzzles by the words (rules) they need, as started in the Language and Categories pages.
  • More optimisation problems in the same frame: timetables, placing facilities, and routing with forbidden neighbours.
